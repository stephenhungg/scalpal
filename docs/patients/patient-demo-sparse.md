### 2.1 Case summary

He presents after 2 days of periumbilical pain that migrated to the RLQ. On day 2 the pain transiently improved (classic for perforation) and then became diffuse, with repeated bilious vomiting, rigors, oliguria and no bowel movement since onset. He is septic: T 38.9, HR 118, BP 102/64, RR 22. He has generalized peritonitis, with involuntary guarding, board-like rigidity and diffuse rebound, maximal in the RLQ. WBC is 18.6 with 12% bands, CRP 180, lactate 2.8 and Cr 1.3 (prerenal). CT shows perforated appendicitis with a periappendiceal phlegmon and free fluid, with no drainable abscess and no free air. **Only by asking him does the learner find his penicillin allergy** (childhood hives).

### 2.2 Diagnosis and why

**Perforated appendicitis with generalized peritonitis and sepsis.** The evidence:
- The classic migration followed by the "lull" (pain relief when the appendix perforates and the distension resolves), then diffuse peritonism.
- Duration over 48 hours.
- High fever, bandemia, raised lactate.
- CT confirmation.

Although phlegmon or abscess alone can be managed non-operatively in stable patients, **generalized peritonitis and sepsis are indications for emergency source control** (WSES 2020): laparoscopic (or open) appendectomy with peritoneal lavage, after resuscitation and immediate antibiotics.

### 2.5 Chart-driven risks and how they change the plan

- **Sparse chart (data gaps: missing medications, allergies, history).** This is the whole point of the case. The chart is not evidence of absence, so confirm identity, allergies, medications and fasting directly at the time-out. Without the interview, the penicillin allergy would be invisible.
- **Penicillin allergy (childhood, non-severe hives, remote, unverified).** It changes antibiotic selection for a complicated intra-abdominal infection:
  - **Avoid piperacillin-tazobactam** (a penicillin).
  - Per the 2022 AAAAI/ACAAI drug allergy practice parameter, a **cephalosporin can be given without testing** for an unverified non-anaphylactic penicillin history. That makes **ceftriaxone plus metronidazole** (SIS 2017 lower-risk regimen) reasonable.
  - For a *serious* beta-lactam allergy, SIS recommends ciprofloxacin plus metronidazole or moxifloxacin.
  - Antibiotics should go in **now**, before the OR. Refer him for penicillin allergy delabeling after recovery.
- **Sepsis (SIRS: T, HR, RR, WBC; lactate 2.8; soft BP).**
  - Start IV crystalloid resuscitation (Surviving Sepsis: about 30 mL/kg for hypoperfusion or lactate ≥4 is the classic anchor; titrate here) and blood cultures.
  - Start broad antibiotics within 1 hour.
  - Place a urinary catheter and monitor urine output (oliguria, Cr 1.3).
  - Check and replace electrolytes (K 3.6 with vomiting).
  - Anesthesia should expect hemodynamic lability at induction.
- **Aspiration risk:** He is vomiting and has an ileus despite >24 h since solids. Use rapid sequence induction and consider an NG tube.
- **AKI (Cr 1.3, prerenal):** Avoid further NSAIDs and nephrotoxins, and dose drugs accordingly. Ibuprofen yesterday plus dehydration is a contributor.
- **Smoking:** Raises the risk of pulmonary complications and wound infection. Offer cessation support after surgery.
- **Alcohol (weekend binge, last drink about 6 days ago):** Withdrawal risk is low but worth documenting.

### 2.7 Socratic probes for common misses

- **Didn't ask about allergies ("chart says none"):** "Does an empty allergy list mean no allergies? What did you just almost give him?"
- **Named appendicitis but not perforation:** "Something happened yesterday afternoon when the pain eased for an hour. What does that tell you?" And: "Is his belly localized or generalized?"
- **Didn't recognize sepsis:** "Read me his vitals again. What do they add up to, and what do you start before the OR?"
- **Proposed antibiotics and interval appendectomy because of the phlegmon:** "When is non-operative management reasonable for a phlegmon, and does this patient fit?"
- **Chose pip-tazo:** "Pip-tazo is a great intra-abdominal drug. What's the problem for *him*? And is every cephalosporin off the table too?"
- **No last-meal or vomiting discussion:** "He's been vomiting all night. What does that mean for induction?"
- **Ignored the creatinine:** "Creatinine 1.3 in a healthy 30-year-old. Why, and what do you avoid?"
- **No lactate or BMP ordered:** "How would you grade how sick he is with one blood test?"

### 2.8 Teaching points

1. **An empty chart is a data gap, not a clean history.** The interview is the safety check.
2. The **pain-free interval** after appendiceal perforation is a classic trap, followed by diffuse peritonitis.
3. Generalized peritonitis plus sepsis means **emergency source control** after rapid resuscitation and antibiotics. Phlegmon or abscess *without* generalized peritonitis is a different (often non-operative) pathway (WSES 2020).
4. **Penicillin allergy labels:** most are not true allergies. Remote non-severe reactions do not preclude cephalosporins (2022 practice parameter). Avoid penicillins, and arrange delabeling later.
5. SIS 2017 empiric regimens for community-acquired intra-abdominal infection: ceftriaxone plus metronidazole for lower risk; fluoroquinolone plus metronidazole for serious beta-lactam allergy.
6. Vomiting and ileus mean a full stomach regardless of fasting time, so use rapid sequence induction.
7. Delayed presentation in young men who "don't do doctors" is a real driver of perforation rates.
