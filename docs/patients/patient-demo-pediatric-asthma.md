## 2. CLINICIAN KEY (never given to the patient agent; for Scalpal and authors)

### 2.1 Case summary

Theo is a 9-year-old, 30 kg boy with mild persistent asthma (on an inhaled corticosteroid plus montelukast, with one ED visit for an exacerbation in November 2024), atopic dermatitis, allergic rhinitis, and peanut allergy with prior anaphylaxis (carries an EpiPen). He presents with about 18 hours of periumbilical pain that has migrated to the RLQ, with anorexia, one episode of vomiting, and a temperature of 38.0. He is tachycardic at 112. He has RLQ tenderness at McBurney's point with rebound, guarding, and positive Rovsing and psoas signs. His testicular exam is normal and his chest is clear. WBC is 14.2 with 85% neutrophils and CRP is 32. Ultrasound shows a noncompressible 9 mm appendix with periappendiceal fluid and hyperemia and no abscess.

### 2.2 Diagnosis and why

**Acute uncomplicated appendicitis.** The evidence:
- Classic migration, with anorexia and vomiting following the pain.
- Fever, leukocytosis with neutrophilia, and a raised CRP.
- Localized peritoneal signs.
- A diagnostic ultrasound: a noncompressible appendix over 6 mm with hyperemia.

His Pediatric Appendicitis Score is about 9–10 of 10 (pain with cough, hop or percussion 2, anorexia 1, fever 1, nausea/vomiting 1, RLQ tenderness 2, WBC >10k 1, ANC >7.5k 1, migration 1), which is high risk. Ultrasound first is correct in a child. CT is not needed.

### 2.3 Must-not-miss differential (discriminating features)

| Diagnosis | Why consider | What argues against here |
|---|---|---|
| **Testicular torsion** | Boys with lower abdominal pain may hide scrotal pain | Normal testicular exam, normal cremasteric reflex. **Must be examined.** |
| Mesenteric adenitis | Common mimic in school-age children, often after a viral illness | No recent viral illness; ultrasound shows an abnormal appendix |
| Gastroenteritis | Vomiting, abdominal pain | Pain came before vomiting; one vomit only; no diarrhea; localized peritonism |
| Constipation | Common cause of pediatric abdominal pain | Normal stool yesterday; fever and peritoneal signs |
| Intussusception | Classic in children | Usually under 3 years; colicky pain, not constant; no currant-jelly stool |
| UTI | Fever, abdominal pain | Normal urinalysis, no urinary symptoms |
| Right lower lobe pneumonia | Referred abdominal pain, especially in asthmatics | Clear chest, no cough, normal work of breathing, SpO2 98% |
| Meckel's diverticulitis | Clinically identical | Appendix is abnormal on ultrasound. If a normal appendix is found at laparoscopy, run the bowel. |

### 2.5 Chart-driven risks and how they change the plan

- **Asthma (chart: mild persistent asthma, ED visit for acute exacerbation 2024-11-03 with SpO2 94% and RR 30; follow-up 2025-03-22 with SpO2 97% and RR 22).** This is a perioperative respiratory adverse event risk.
  - Assess control now: rescue use 2 days ago, no URI in the past 4 weeks, chest clear.
  - Continue controllers. Give albuterol before induction (per anesthesia), and have it available.
  - Pneumoperitoneum raises airway pressures, so use a lower insufflation pressure in children.
  - Mother's "never hospitalized" is true (the ED visit ended in discharge). The ED visit still counts as a significant exacerbation within 2 years and should be in the presentation.
- **Peanut allergy with anaphylaxis (chart: high criticality; peanut IgE 15.2 kU/L; total IgE 412).** This is not a drug allergy, but it must be in the time-out:
  - The team should know it, he should have an allergy band, and food must be peanut-free after surgery.
  - The chart has no epinephrine auto-injector on its medication list, so reconcile with the parent.
  - **Propofol is not contraindicated** in peanut, soy or egg allergy (BJA 2019 review; Pediatric Anesthesia 2019).
  - Atopy is a modest general risk factor for perioperative hypersensitivity.
- **Atopic background (eczema, allergic rhinitis, eosinophils 0.7).** Contributes to airway reactivity.
- **Pediatric flag:**
  - Weight-based dosing at 30 kg.
  - A smaller working space means lower pneumoperitoneum pressure.
  - A parent should be present for induction where policy allows.
  - Use child-life support and topical anesthetic for IV access.
- **Antibiotics:** Standard for uncomplicated appendicitis (for example, cefazolin plus metronidazole, or ceftriaxone plus metronidazole per protocol). There is no drug allergy.
- **Fasting:** Clears until about 1 hour ago. Many pediatric centers now accept clears up to 1 hour before induction, but local policy varies. Solids (crackers) were at 19:00 the night before. Tell anesthesia.
- **Analgesia:** Give it now. Analgesia does not mask the diagnosis. NSAIDs are a consideration in asthma: most asthmatic children tolerate them, but ask about prior NSAID-triggered bronchospasm before giving ibuprofen or ketorolac.

### 2.7 Socratic probes for common misses

- **No testicular exam:** "Nine-year-old boy, lower abdominal pain. What's the one exam you'd never want to explain skipping?"
- **Asthma not explored:** "His mother says 'well controlled.' What three questions turn that into something anesthesia can use?" (rescue use, recent URI, prior ED/ICU/intubation)
- **Peanut allergy dismissed as "not a drug allergy":** "Does a food allergy matter in the operating room? Who needs to know, and what about the propofol myth?"
- **Ordered CT first:** "Why might a pediatric surgeon frown at that CT order?"
- **Chest not examined:** "Which chest problem can look exactly like this belly, especially in an asthmatic kid?"
- **No last-meal history:** "Anesthesia wants to induce in an hour. What do you tell them?"
- **Withheld analgesia:** "He's crying in pain. Will morphine hide the diagnosis?"
- **Missed the chart discrepancy:** "The chart says hives and lip swelling at age two. Mom says anaphylaxis at five. Which one do you plan around?"

### 2.8 Teaching points

1. **Ultrasound first in children**. Reserve CT for an equivocal ultrasound.
2. The **testicular exam** is mandatory in boys with lower abdominal pain.
3. Asthma risk stratification before anesthesia: control, recent exacerbations, URI within 4 weeks, prior intubation or ICU, and rescue use. "Never hospitalized" is not the same as "no ED visits."
4. Food allergy belongs in the time-out. Propofol is safe in peanut, soy and egg allergy.
5. PAS and Alvarado scores support but do not replace clinical judgment and imaging.
6. Early analgesia is safe and humane.
7. The history giver is a parent: verify allergies and medications with the parent *and* the chart, and reconcile any gaps (here, the EpiPen is missing from the chart med list).
